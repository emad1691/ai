Approach

Design that starts with what
the land already knows.

Most design problems in landscape architecture are analysis problems in disguise. Get the site reading right - hydrology, microclimate, succession, culture - and the design decisions tend to follow with a kind of inevitability.

My studio practice runs between hand drawing and digital tools because I find the handoff between scales forces clear thinking. A sketch cannot lie about proportion. A grading plan cannot fake the drainage logic.

I'm building a practice that takes the Mountain West seriously on its own terms - its dryness, its altitude, its particular light - rather than importing design language from somewhere else.

Ecological Design · Studio III

Riparian Corridor
Restoration

Context

A degraded riparian corridor along Boulder Creek had been treated as flood infrastructure for decades - channelized, constrained, and cut off from surrounding neighborhoods.

Objective

Restore the creek corridor's ecological function while transforming it into a continuous public greenway. Flood resilience and community amenity as the same move.

Strategy & Execution

Site analysis traced existing meander patterns, soil types, and riparian plant communities. Design language was drawn directly from creek geomorphology - sinuous paths mirroring meander geometry, canopy structures echoing cottonwood form, bioswales reading as restored oxbow features. Native planting palette organized by flood frequency zone. Grading designed to detain the 100-year event within the corridor itself.

Takeaway

The strongest design decisions were ecological ones. When you let hydrology drive the layout, the human experience follows almost automatically - the path goes where the water logic takes it.

Site Design · Studio II

Alpine Threshold:
Rocky Mountain Gateway

Context

A trailhead entry zone at the montane-to-subalpine transition - high visibility from the road, exposed to severe weather, heavy foot traffic into a sensitive wilderness boundary.

Objective

Design a minimal structure that manages vehicle arrival without announcing itself - infrastructure that performs without competing with the landscape it serves.

Strategy & Execution

Embed the structure into existing topography so it disappears on approach and reveals itself only at the threshold moment of entry. The drama is choreographed, not visual. Material selection: weathering steel, local granite, and sedum roofing programmed to colonize over three to five years. Grading designed around a low stone retaining wall that reads as natural rockfall from the road.

Takeaway

The best infrastructure at elevation is the kind that earns its place by becoming invisible. Restraint is a design choice, not a lack of one.

Landscape Analysis · Site Documentation

Mesa Siting Study:
Wind & Shadow

Context

A 40-acre mesa west of Boulder proposed for low-density development - exposed tableland, significant wind loading, complex seasonal shadow patterns from adjacent peaks.

Objective

Produce an environmental baseline analysis detailed enough to drive defensible siting decisions for any built elements on the mesa.

Strategy & Execution

Layered analysis across six variables: seasonal sun angles, prevailing wind vectors, drainage patterns, soil horizons, vegetation communities, and existing views. Each layer mapped independently, then overlaid to identify where constraints align and where they create genuine conflict. GIS analysis for spatial work; hand-drawn wind roses and sun path diagrams for non-technical client presentation.

Takeaway

Analysis is a design act. The layering decisions - what to include, what weight to give each variable - shape the conclusions as much as the data does.

Urban Landscape · Collaborative Studio

CU Boulder
South Campus Commons

Context

An underused service corridor on CU Boulder's south campus - functionally invisible to students, ecologically unmanaged, missing an opportunity to connect two active zones of campus life.

Objective

Transform the corridor into a daily-use student commons that also functions as a stormwater management system. Dual accountability to people and infrastructure.

Strategy & Execution

Landform terracing to create differentiated use zones without hard edges. Bioretention integrated into the landform geometry rather than treated as separate utility infrastructure. My role: grading strategy and planting design - selecting a dry-land prairie palette for Boulder's semi-arid climate. Four-person team; I developed the grading plan, cross-sections, and planting legend.

Takeaway

Collaborative design is faster when everyone owns a specific discipline. Ambiguity about who decides what is where most team projects lose time.

About
Emilio

Environmental Design Student · Landscape Architecture · CU Boulder

"I'm drawn to places that have strong opinions about themselves."

I'm Emilio Adams - a third-year Environmental Design student at CU Boulder concentrating in landscape architecture, expected to graduate May 2026. I grew up between the high desert of New Mexico and the Front Range of Colorado, which means I've been thinking since early on about what makes a landscape feel inevitable rather than imposed.

My design approach is grounded in site analysis first. Before any design move, I want to understand what a place already knows - how water moves through it, where the wind comes from, what has been growing there long enough to belong. Most of my strongest design decisions have started as observations, not ideas.

In the studio I work across hand drawing, physical models, GIS, AutoCAD, and Rhino. Analog and digital tools reveal different things - a 1:50 section sketch shows you proportion, a drainage model shows you consequence - and moving between them is where the design thinking actually happens.

Outside the studio I spend significant time in the field. Not documenting in an academic sense - just being in landscapes, paying attention to how they feel at different hours and seasons. I care about what it feels like to inhabit a place, not just what it looks like on plan.

I'm actively looking for a summer 2026 internship or entry-level opportunity with a landscape architecture or environmental planning firm working in the Mountain West - ecological restoration, public land design, and community-scale open space work.

Current Direction

Moving toward firms doing serious ecological work in the Mountain West - restoration, public land, and open space at community scale. Less interested in purely decorative landscape; more interested in sites with real ecological or hydrological problems to solve. Open to studio environments that value technical rigor and clear representation alongside design quality.
